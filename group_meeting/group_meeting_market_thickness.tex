\noindent\textbf{Preview.}
A publicly known single driver gives an exact cutoff-WPBE and a closed-form
optimal menu.  With latent Poisson supply, the completion value of rescue
pricing is small in thin markets, largest at an intermediate thickness, and
small again in thick markets.  For
$(\alpha,\beta,\delta,\gamma)=(1,.8,.8,0)$, the deterministic numerical
solution peaks near $m=9.72$ and improves optimized completion by $6.16$
percentage points.  The endpoint pattern is proved; strict single-peakedness
is a conjecture.

\section{Exact one-rider/one-driver benchmark}

\subsection{Closed-form solution}

The rider has $v\sim U[0,1]$, the driver has $c\sim U[0,1]$, and the platform
maximizes completion.  Delayed rider value is $\beta v$ and delayed incumbent
surplus is $\delta(p_j-c)$.  The platform commits to
$0\le p_1\le p_2\le1$.  The number of drivers is publicly known to be exactly
one; there is no survival shock or fresh entry.

For an active menu $0\le p_1<p_2<\beta$, the driver accepts immediately iff
$c\le\ch$.  After rejection, the rider cancels, repeats $p_1$, or activates
$p_2$ according to
\[
v<\frac{p_1}{\beta},\qquad
\frac{p_1}{\beta}\le v\le v^M(\ch),\qquad
v>v^M(\ch),
\qquad
v^M(\ch)=\frac{p_1+p_2-\ch}{\beta}.
\]
The marginal driver's accept-versus-wait indifference gives the WPBE cutoff
\begin{equation}
\ch(p_1,p_2)=\left[p_1-
\frac{\delta(p_2-p_1)(\beta-p_2)}
{\beta(1-p_1)-\delta(\beta-p_2)}\right]_+ ,
\tag{1}
\end{equation}
and equilibrium completion is
\begin{equation}
M_1(p_1,p_2)=(1-p_1)\ch+
\frac{(\beta-p_2)(p_2-\ch)}{\beta}.
\tag{2}
\end{equation}
Thus the cutoff is calculated from the WPBE; the platform then optimizes over
menus subject to this equilibrium response.

Fixing a target cutoff reduces the two-price problem to one dimension.  The
unique continuation envelope and its implementing first price are
\begin{equation}
\widehat p_2(\ch)=\frac{\beta+\ch}{2},\qquad
\widehat p_1(\ch)=
\frac{1+\ch-\sqrt{\Delta(\ch)}}2,\qquad
\Delta(\ch)=(1-\ch)^2-\frac{\delta}{\beta}(\beta-\ch)^2.
\tag{3}
\end{equation}
Substitution into (2) gives
\begin{equation}
M_1^*=\max\left\{\frac14,\ \max_{\ch\in[0,\beta]}J_\delta(\ch)\right\},
\quad
J_\delta(\ch)=
\frac{\ch[1-\ch+\sqrt{\Delta(\ch)}]}2
+\frac{(\beta-\ch)^2}{4\beta}.
\tag{4}
\end{equation}
If $\beta\le1/2$ or $\delta=1$, the optimal value is the flat benchmark
$1/4$.  If $\beta>1/2$ and $\delta<1$, $J_\delta$ is strictly concave and has
a unique interior maximizer, which implements $p_1^*<p_2^*$.

\subsection{Numerical illustration}

At $\beta=\delta=4/5$, the first-order condition becomes
$12-20\ch=5\ch\sqrt{9-10\ch}$.  Squaring yields
$(5\ch-4)(50\ch^2+75\ch-36)=0$; checking the unsquared equation and
feasibility leaves the positive root of the quadratic.  Its discriminant is
$75^2+4(50)(36)=225\cdot57$, which explains the appearance of $\sqrt{57}$:
\[
\ch^*=\frac{3(\sqrt{57}-5)}{20}=.382475,\quad
p_1^*=\frac{11+\sqrt{57}}{40}=.463746,\quad
p_2^*=\frac{1+3\sqrt{57}}{40}=.591238.
\]
The optimal rescue menu completes $25.9581\%$ of requests, compared with
$25.0000\%$ under the best flat price.

\clearpage
\section{Main numerical result: too thin, just right, too thick}

\subsection{The object we compare}

From this page onward the realized incumbent count is latent:
$N^I\sim\operatorname{Pois}(m)$.  Hence $m=1$ below is \emph{not} the
publicly known $N=1$ benchmark on the first two pages.  At the same thickness,
\[
M_R^*(m)=\max_{p_1\le p_2}M_m(p_1,p_2),\qquad
M_F^*(m)=\max_pM_m(p,p),\qquad
V(m)=M_R^*(m)-M_F^*(m).
\]
Thus $100V(m)$ is the percentage-point value of allowing an announced rescue
price, holding the market fixed.  Every point is a deterministic equilibrium
optimization, not Monte Carlo output.  The baseline is
$F=G=U[0,1]$ and $(\alpha,\beta,\delta,\gamma)=(1,.8,.8,0)$.

\vspace{-4pt}
\caption{The full numerical profile.  Panels (a)--(c) globally re-optimize the

\label{fig:profiles}
\vspace{-4pt}

\takeaway{Rescue is a just-right instrument.  If the market is too thin,
there is almost nobody left to rescue.  If it is too thick, flat pricing
already completes almost every request.  The opportunity is in the middle:
failure still happens, but failure leaves a useful screened backup pool.  In
the baseline, the numerical maximum is $6.16$ pp at $m=9.72$.}

\subsection{Why ten drivers later is not a sufficient statistic}

Universal rejection screens incumbents: the survivors have $c>\ch$.  Fresh
entrants are unscreened draws from $F$.  Even if two continuation pools have
the same expected headcount, they induce different waiting today.

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabularx}{.96\textwidth}{@{}L{3.1cm}XX@{}}
\toprule
Who is in the backup pool? & Effect on today's cutoff & Effect after failure\\
\midrule
More screened incumbents & Stronger strategic erosion: today's drivers own
the future option & Can help or hurt as $\delta$ changes\\
More unscreened entrants & No first-window response from the new drivers &
Adds competition, but can make rescue unnecessary\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

\noindent The completion ranking can therefore flip with driver patience even
when continuation headcount or price-marginal capacity is held fixed.  This is
the economically relevant composition result.

\section{What makes the plots credible: the short theorem chain}

\takeaway{The optimizer is not drawing a nice curve from a price grid.  It

In the uniform no-entry core this entire equilibrium-constrained problem is

one-dimensional global design $\rightarrow$ thickness endpoints.  Concave
supply, multiplicity, and mixed entry then appear as boundary tests.  This
keeps the central claim readable and prevents local extensions from being
mistaken for global theorems.

\noindent\textbf{Bottom line.}
The closest synthesis is Correa's commitment and waiting, Li--Kuo's latent
Poisson competition, Lee--Li's continuation composition, and Hu--Hu--Zhu's
two-sided temporal response.  The incremental object is their \emph{joint}
equilibrium-design structure under public failure, together with the optimized
completion comparison against flat pricing.
